The playground is the full application — the same runtime, compiled to WebAssembly, running entirely in your browser. Nothing you make here leaves your machine unless you ask a model about it.

A short history

🍎 In 1987, Apple shipped HyperCard, and software stopped being something only programmers made.

You drew a button, typed two lines of plain English, and that was the app. No project file. No compiler. Sounds familiar?

  • 👩‍🏫 Teachers wrote their own courseware
  • 🏛️ Museums ran kiosks on it
  • 🏝️ Myst was made in it

It came free on every Mac for over a decade. Then it was gone, and nothing quite took its place.

HyperLab is what that idea looks like thirty years on, with language models built into the way a stack thinks rather than bolted onto the side of one. Draw a button, write two lines of HyperTalk — or ask the assistant to do it for you, and undo it like any other change.

🕯️ Dedicated to Bill Atkinson (1951–2025), who wrote QuickDraw, MacPaint and HyperCard, and insisted the last of them ship free with every Mac.

Six stacks to open

Every example opens in the playground, running. Click things. Then switch to the edit tool and read the scripts that made them work — the two qualities that made the original worth learning.

  • Cluedo A drawn mansion with clickable rooms, and a case you close by deduction.
  • Myst An island, four Ages, and one book you should not open. Go ▸ Map finds the trap without running a line.
  • LLMs for n00bs Nine slides on how a language model works. The last one asks a real model, and says so plainly when none is set up.
  • Address Book A card per person, one shared background.
  • Recipe Box Chunk expressions doing arithmetic on the ingredients.
  • Todo A list, a dialog, and a loop.

Bring your own model, or none

HyperLab works with no AI provider configured, and always will. When you want one, open AI ▸ Settings in the playground and add Anthropic, OpenAI, OpenRouter, an Ollama on your own machine — or the built-in mock, which answers locally and needs no key at all.

Your API key stays in your browser. This site is static files on GitHub Pages — there is no HyperLab server, so there is nowhere for a key to be sent. A key you type in is kept in this browser's storage on your device and travels exactly one way: with the requests your stack makes, straight from your browser to the provider you chose. There is no call that reads a key back out, and the sidebar shows the exact text of everything that was sent.

The assistant can only do what you can do — it works the runtime's own commands, so everything it makes is visible, inspectable, and undoable.

Take it home

The desktop application — a .dmg, an .msi, and a .deb, .rpm or .AppImage — is on the releases page. Nothing phones home. A stack you download from the playground opens in the app, and the other way round: it is the same file.

A stack can also leave for somewhere else entirely: the cards as a PDF, the map as a PNG, the whole thing as a self-contained web page, or as a Decker deck.

HyperLab is MIT-licensed and contains no Apple code and no Apple artwork; the resemblance is to the era, not to any particular thing. Built with Rust, WebAssembly, and affection for 1987.
